I am a PhD student in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Nanyang Technological University, under the supervision of Prof. Rong Su. My research is based at the Centre for System Intelligence and Efficiency (CSIE).

Research at NTU · 01

Multi-Agent System Autonomy

Distributed control · Coordination · Information exchange · Adaptive behavior

Concept illustration · Autonomous coordination among locally informed agents

Research objective

I investigate how a group of agents can coordinate its behavior through local sensing, information exchange, and feedback control. The goal is to connect individual decision-making with a shared objective while allowing the group to respond to changes in its task or environment.

Questions I am exploring

  • What information should agents exchange to support coordinated action?
  • How can local decisions maintain a coherent collective structure as conditions change?
  • How can task reasoning, role allocation, and physical control be connected in a feedback process?

Research approach

My current work connects distributed estimation and graph-based coordination with task-dependent control objectives. A complementary direction investigates a three-layer architecture linking information updates, local task reasoning, and execution through coupled agent-level feedback.

The emphasis is on interpretable mechanisms: what an agent knows, why it communicates, and how its control action contributes to the collective task.

Research at NTU · 02

Swarm Intelligence

Collective intelligence · Knowledge evolution · Local interactions · Adaptation

Concept illustration · Individual agents and emergent group-level intelligence

Research objective

I study why a swarm can exhibit intelligence, how this intelligence can be realized through individual agents, and what internal logic connects local interactions with group-level capability.

My aim is to identify the mechanisms through which information exchange, reasoning, and evolving knowledge generate collective intelligence, and to construct and evaluate these mechanisms in multi-agent systems.

Questions I am exploring

  • How does an agent’s knowledge change through communication and experience?
  • How do patterns of interaction influence collective understanding and adaptation?
  • How can individual capability, interactive capability, and group intelligence be distinguished and evaluated?

Research approach

I explore representations of local information and knowledge, interpretable rules for interaction, and feedback between collective outcomes and subsequent decisions. This connects the study of individual reasoning with the design of communication structures and group-level adaptation.

The intended outcome is a clearer account of why a group becomes more capable through interaction, together with mechanisms that can be examined mathematically and in multi-agent simulations.

Independent research & applications · 03

Football & Collective Coordination

Control theory · Game theory · Tactical analysis · Relational formations

Concept illustration · Relational coordination and tactical structure in football

Research objective

I study football as a dynamic multi-agent system in which team organization emerges through player movement, role assignment, and tactical interaction. My broader interest is to use control theory, game theory, and network science to explain how individual actions shape collective structure.

Questions I am exploring

  • How do changes in a player’s position or role affect the organization of the team?
  • How can local coordination preserve tactically meaningful spatial relationships?
  • How can formation structure and movement be evaluated in relation to a tactical objective?

Current work

The current formation-control formulation uses task-conditioned relational objectives, tactical graphs, and role-dependent feasibility constraints. Its nominal analysis considers a fixed tactical mode and common formation reference, with an 11-player formation as the simulation setting.

The broader research direction extends to interpreting tactical adaptation and coordinated player movement. These broader questions are distinct from the scope of the current fixed-task analysis.

Interactive research & development

OWN Tactic translates this research direction into an interactive environment. Version 1 establishes the formation-control foundation: users can perturb player positions, configure tactical and communication graphs, and compare recovery, coverage, movement, and defensive response under controlled settings.

Version 2 expands the experiment from a team recovering a prescribed structure toward a richer two-team setting. It asks how tactical reference, ball position, opponent pressure, teammate information, communication, and task priority can be combined into interpretable player-level decisions—and how those decisions accumulate into team behavior.

For me, this is where mathematical control meets the emotional intelligence of football: rigorous enough to expose mechanisms and assumptions, but open enough to challenge them through movement, pressure, disruption, and tactical imagination.

VERSION 2 · INTERPRETABLE MECHANISMS

From individual control to collective tactics

Version 2 is organized around a control-oriented question: if every player responds only to locally meaningful signals, what structures of capability and communication are sufficient to produce coherent tactical behavior? The interface makes these relationships visible so that controller choices can be inspected rather than hidden behind animation.

Local controller & player decisions

A player is modeled as a locally informed agent. Tactical tracking, formation maintenance, ball-oriented motion, opponent response, neighbor coordination, and task attraction contribute distinct terms to the movement decision. This decomposition provides a basis for discussing not only what the player does, but why the controller produces that action.

Network structure & collective behavior

Sensing range, communication radius, information reliability, tactical influence, and local responsibility determine how information and control effects propagate across a team. Version 2 uses these quantities as design and observation variables for exploring when local coordination becomes stable, responsive, or tactically meaningful.

Research interpretation. These visualizations describe the platform’s modeling and experimental logic. Claims about convergence, stability, or tactical performance remain tied to explicit assumptions, metrics, and separate mathematical or numerical validation.

Independent research & applications · 04

Networked Economy & Collective Estimation

Heterogeneous partial information · Distributed estimation · Social consultation · Knowledge assimilation · Network topology

Research motivation

No individual agent sees the whole economy. Each has access to a different subset of signals, develops a local model from that partial view, and carries its own limitations in both information and understanding. I use economic estimation—currently centered on inflation—as a concrete setting for asking a broader systems question: how can heterogeneous, partially informed agents learn from one another without assuming an omniscient coordinator?

The primary contribution I am working toward is therefore not an economic forecast. It is an interpretable framework for studying distributed estimation, information complementarity, network structure, and the relationship between individual and group intelligence.

Questions I am exploring

  • What can an agent infer from a small set of locally available signals?
  • Which neighbors possess genuinely complementary information or knowledge?
  • When does consultation improve an individual estimate, and when does it merely reproduce redundant or unreliable information?
  • How does communication topology change both the average group outcome and the distribution of capability across agents?

From private cognition to collective estimation

The platform organizes the research logic into four connected stages. The stages make it possible to separate what an agent knows alone from what becomes possible through structured interaction.

01

Private Cognition

Each agent forms a local estimator from its own limited set of economic signals. This private baseline preserves the heterogeneity of the population.

02

Social Consultation

An agent consults selected neighbors through a designed communication graph, gaining access to targeted estimates or information rather than a complete global state.

03

Knowledge Assimilation

Received knowledge is evaluated and selectively incorporated according to its reliability, complementarity, and relevance to the receiving agent.

04

Collective Estimation

Private and post-consultation outcomes are compared at both the individual and population levels to examine where collective intelligence emerges—and where it does not.

Information propagation & network topology

A network determines which knowledge can become accessible, but access does not imply automatic adoption. OWN2 treats topology, consultation, and assimilation as distinct mechanisms: connections create opportunities to communicate; agent-level judgment determines whether received information is useful; collective metrics reveal how these local choices accumulate across the population.

OWN2 Collective Studio

OWN2 translates the framework into a hands-on research sandbox. Visitors can create and configure agents, assign local economic signals, draw a communication graph, and compare what the system produces before and after consultation.

  • Define heterogeneous agents and their local information.
  • Construct communication links and experiment with network structure.
  • Run private, consulted, assimilated, and collective estimation stages.
  • Compare individual and group-level outcomes.
  • Save and submit a design for later comparison or discussion.
Launch OWN2 Collective Studio ↗

Scope note. OWN2 is a research and educational prototype for comparative multi-agent experiments. It is not financial forecasting or investment advice. Observed performance depends on the selected data, local models, network topology, interaction rules, and evaluation period; interactive results should not be interpreted as universal economic conclusions.

Purdue University · ECE 270 · 2024

FPGA Lunar Lander

SystemVerilog · Finite-state machines · Sequential logic · FPGA implementation

Project overview

For the ECE 270 final project, I implemented a lunar-lander simulation and its control logic on an FPGA. The project translated a simplified physical system into digital state updates and hardware logic.

Design & implementation

  • Representing altitude, velocity, fuel, and control inputs.
  • Implementing combinational and sequential logic in SystemVerilog.
  • Organizing the design around state transitions and hardware timing.
  • Checking the behavior against the project’s functional requirements.

What this project contributed

This experience connected mathematical system descriptions with executable hardware and strengthened my understanding of how control logic is represented, organized, and tested.

Purdue University · 2025

Optical Heart Rate Sensor

Photoplethysmography · Infrared sensing · Analog filters · Signal conditioning

Project overview

I designed and implemented a photoplethysmography-based optical heart-rate sensor. The project combined optical components, analog circuits, filtering, and signal interpretation into a working measurement chain.

Design & implementation

  • Integrating infrared sensing components and their driving circuits.
  • Using amplification and filtering to extract the pulse-related signal.
  • Managing gain and hysteresis to address noise and small signal amplitudes.
  • Evaluating the sensing chain across the project’s target heart-rate range.

What this project contributed

The project made the connection between a physical phenomenon and its measured signal concrete. It strengthened my understanding of sensing, noise, circuit-level constraints, and system integration.
