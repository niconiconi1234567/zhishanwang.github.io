I am a PhD student in the School of Electrical and Electronic Engineering at Nanyang Technological University, under the supervision of Prof. Rong Su. I am a member of Robot Intelligence & Metacognition Laboratory (RIM Lab), within the Centre for System Intelligence and Efficiency (CSIE).

My research connects information, learning and control in multi-agent systems. I study what locally informed agents can understand, how they acquire complementary knowledge, and how that knowledge becomes coordinated action. Current directions include collective identifiability, learning from observed behavior, and evidence-driven mission planning, with applications in robotics, football and economic estimation.

I received my BS in Electrical Engineering from Purdue University in 2026, graduating with Highest Distinction, with a minor in Mathematics and a concentration in Automatic Control.

RESEARCH AT NTU · MISSION–CONTROL

Knowledge, Missions & Executable Swarm Behavior

Knowledge updates · Task reasoning · Distributed control · Metacognition

How can new evidence change what a robot should do next, and how can that decision remain connected to executable physical behavior?

The research question

A controller can track a reference without knowing whether the reference still serves the mission. This project connects information, mission reasoning and execution inside each agent, so that evidence can change the next task and the resulting motion.

How the ideas connect

01

Information

Collect local measurements, messages and observable teammate behavior with their source, time and object scope.

02

Task / reasoning

Check rule support, identify missing prerequisites and choose a justified task and mission transition.

03

Execution / control

Generate a reference, plan motion and enforce physical constraints; the resulting action changes the evidence available next.

Current progress

The research has progressed from a complementary-knowledge star prototype to three search-and-rescue demonstrations. Demo 0 activates and reuses evidence-supported transition rules; Demo 1 adds joint rescue prerequisites and physical evacuation; Demo 2 creates active information tasks and allocates heterogeneous sensing capabilities.

From rule support to active evidence acquisition

Demo 0

Activate & reuse

Learn which transitions are supported by observations and teammate behavior.

Demo 1

Coordinate & rescue

Combine peer support, joint prerequisites and physical evacuation.

Demo 2

Ask & acquire

Create missing-information tasks and assign agents with complementary sensing.

The next theoretical step is to represent knowledge and rule learning explicitly, beyond activating a fixed catalogue of candidate rules.

Assumptions & scope

The current demonstrations use specified mission modes and executable skills. They illustrate online composition, evidence checking and coordination; they do not establish unrestricted task invention or a general guarantee for every rescue environment.

What comes next

Develop explicit guarantees connecting evidence validity, mission reachability, task repair and closed-loop execution. Extend local checks toward diagnosing model limitations and choosing how to learn.

OWN2 PLUS · CAPABILITIES AND EVIDENCE

Information Formation, Identifiability & Resilience

Heterogeneous capabilities · Executable information · Collective identifiability · Robust acquisition

A connected network does not automatically possess the information needed for its task. What can its agents actually produce, combine and deliver?

The research question

OWN2 begins with partially informed estimators. OWN2 Plus asks the more fundamental question: which state distinctions can become available at all when sensing, local processing, communication, time and failure dependencies are constrained?

How the ideas connect

01

Information formation

Describe the evidence that heterogeneous agents can generate through permitted operations and feasible protocols.

02

Attainable knowledge

Determine which hidden states remain observationally indistinguishable after the available evidence is combined.

03

Reliable acquisition

Choose capabilities and repeated measurements under finite resources and shared physical failure dependencies.

Current progress

The program now separates two connected theory strands. One studies capability-constrained collective identifiability, same-episode information fusion and structural erasure resilience. The other studies failure-resilient information acquisition, robust coverage, finite-budget minimax risk and joint capability installation.

Assumptions & scope

Identifiability of a hidden state is distinct from identifying the communication graph itself. A rank condition must refer to information that is actually executable; an asymptotic error exponent is not the same objective as finite-budget error probability.

What comes next

Connect capability-aware evidence design to distributed observers and mission-level decisions, while testing exactly which communication and failure patterns limit the usable information.

Connected research

Questions for collaboration

Potential directions include capability-aware sensing design, distributed observer construction, and information acquisition under shared failures and finite resource budgets.

GAME-THEORETIC INFORMATION · OGT1

Collective History Identifiability & Causal Attainability

Imperfect information · Heterogeneous sensing · Protocol synthesis · Information theory

A team may collectively hold enough evidence to distinguish two histories, yet no individual may be able to obtain that evidence in time.

The research question

This program starts from heterogeneous information sets in multi-agent interaction. It separates what all observations could reveal in principle from what an agent can learn through a causal communication and acquisition protocol.

How the ideas connect

01

Structural distinction

Identify history pairs that different sensing capabilities can distinguish; characterize the remaining ambiguity.

02

Causal recovery

Construct admissible interaction and communication protocols that make the required distinctions available at the decision point.

03

Decision and control

Ask which distinctions are sufficient for an action, a functional observer or a stabilizing feedback law.

Current progress

The work has developed complementary structural and information-theoretic manuscripts: collective history identifiability and protocol synthesis; heterogeneous sensing with two-way communication; and extensions linking causal capability discovery to observer-based control and mean-square stabilizability.

Assumptions & scope

These are ongoing manuscript and theory developments. Their target venues are not presented as completed submissions. Collective access to all observations is not silently substituted for locally attainable knowledge.

What comes next

Study knowledge-gap discovery, interactive acquisition of complementary evidence and the network-dependent time and resource cost of obtaining control-relevant information.

OWN · LEARNING FROM EXTERNAL BEHAVIOR

The Lonely Observer

Action observations · Hidden feedback · Statistical distinguishability · Strategic participation

What can an outsider understand about an unfamiliar multi-agent system by observing what its members do?

The research question

The observer sees only parts of a group’s motion and executed actions, without direct access to its internal states, feedback laws or communications. The goal is to learn a useful model, understand the limits of that model and decide when the available understanding is sufficient to act.

How the ideas connect

01

Level 1 · control value

Determine when observing actions improves a specified control task, and when finite data provide enough evidence to use a learned predictor.

02

Level 2 · distinguishability

Characterize the observation windows, network structure and action measurements needed to distinguish hidden feedback mechanisms.

03

Level 3 · participation

Study how an entering agent can learn while acting, and how information value interacts with probing costs and strategic response.

Current progress

The first two levels now have dedicated theory manuscripts. Level 1 separates richer dynamical information from actual control benefit. Level 2 studies finite-window identifiability, statistical reliability and action-sensing design. Level 3 is being developed through explicit models of participation under incomplete information.

Current theory manuscripts

Assumptions & scope

Observing more hidden modes need not improve control. Passive prediction of existing behavior need not identify the response to a new intervention. The three levels retain these distinctions instead of treating learning, identification and decision quality as interchangeable.

What comes next

Develop decision-sufficient understanding and test how well behavior-based models transfer when the observer becomes a participant in the system.

Multi-Agent System Autonomy

Distributed control · Coordination · Information exchange · Adaptive behavior

Concept illustration · Autonomous coordination among locally informed agents

Research objective

I investigate how a group of agents can coordinate its behavior through local sensing, information exchange, and feedback control. The goal is to connect individual decision-making with a shared objective while allowing the group to respond to changes in its task or environment.

Questions I am exploring

  • What information should agents exchange to support coordinated action?
  • How can local decisions maintain a coherent collective structure as conditions change?
  • How can task reasoning, role allocation, and physical control be connected in a feedback process?

Research approach

My current work connects distributed estimation and graph-based coordination with task-dependent control objectives. A complementary direction investigates a three-layer architecture linking information updates, local task reasoning, and execution through coupled agent-level feedback.

The emphasis is on interpretable mechanisms: what an agent knows, why it communicates, and how its control action contributes to the collective task.

Research at NTU · 02

Swarm Intelligence

Collective intelligence · Knowledge evolution · Local interactions · Adaptation

Concept illustration · Individual agents and emergent group-level intelligence

Research objective

I study why a swarm can exhibit intelligence, how this intelligence can be realized through individual agents, and what internal logic connects local interactions with group-level capability.

My aim is to identify the mechanisms through which information exchange, reasoning, and evolving knowledge generate collective intelligence, and to construct and evaluate these mechanisms in multi-agent systems.

Questions I am exploring

  • How does an agent’s knowledge change through communication and experience?
  • How do patterns of interaction influence collective understanding and adaptation?
  • How can individual capability, interactive capability, and group intelligence be distinguished and evaluated?

Research approach

I explore representations of local information and knowledge, interpretable rules for interaction, and feedback between collective outcomes and subsequent decisions. This connects the study of individual reasoning with the design of communication structures and group-level adaptation.

The intended outcome is a clearer account of why a group becomes more capable through interaction, together with mechanisms that can be examined mathematically and in multi-agent simulations.

Independent research & applications · 03

Football & Collective Coordination

Control theory · Game theory · Tactical analysis · Relational formations

Concept illustration · Relational coordination and tactical structure in football

Research objective

I study football as a dynamic multi-agent system in which team organization emerges through player movement, role assignment, and tactical interaction. My broader interest is to use control theory, game theory, and network science to explain how individual actions shape collective structure.

Questions I am exploring

  • How do changes in a player’s position or role affect the organization of the team?
  • How can local coordination preserve tactically meaningful spatial relationships?
  • How can formation structure and movement be evaluated in relation to a tactical objective?

Current work

The current formation-control formulation uses task-conditioned relational objectives, tactical graphs, and role-dependent feasibility constraints. Its nominal analysis considers a fixed tactical mode and common formation reference, with an 11-player formation as the simulation setting.

The broader research direction extends to interpreting tactical adaptation and coordinated player movement. These broader questions are distinct from the scope of the current fixed-task analysis.

Interactive research & development

OWN Tactic translates this research direction into an interactive environment. Version 1 establishes the formation-control foundation: users can perturb player positions, configure tactical and communication graphs, and compare recovery, coverage, movement, and defensive response under controlled settings.

Version 2 expands the experiment from a team recovering a prescribed structure toward a richer two-team setting. It asks how tactical reference, ball position, opponent pressure, teammate information, communication, and task priority can be combined into interpretable player-level decisions—and how those decisions accumulate into team behavior.

For me, this is where mathematical control meets the emotional intelligence of football: rigorous enough to expose mechanisms and assumptions, but open enough to challenge them through movement, pressure, disruption, and tactical imagination.

VERSION 2 · INTERPRETABLE MECHANISMS

From individual control to collective tactics

Version 2 is organized around a control-oriented question: if every player responds only to locally meaningful signals, what structures of capability and communication are sufficient to produce coherent tactical behavior? The interface makes these relationships visible so that controller choices can be inspected rather than hidden behind animation.

Local controller & player decisions

A player is modeled as a locally informed agent. Tactical tracking, formation maintenance, ball-oriented motion, opponent response, neighbor coordination, and task attraction contribute distinct terms to the movement decision. This decomposition provides a basis for discussing not only what the player does, but why the controller produces that action.

Network structure & collective behavior

Sensing range, communication radius, information reliability, tactical influence, and local responsibility determine how information and control effects propagate across a team. Version 2 uses these quantities as design and observation variables for exploring when local coordination becomes stable, responsive, or tactically meaningful.

Independent research & applications · 04

Networked Economy & Collective Estimation

Heterogeneous partial information · Distributed estimation · Social consultation · Knowledge assimilation · Network topology

Research motivation

No individual agent sees the whole economy. Each has access to a different subset of signals, develops a local model from that partial view, and carries its own limitations in both information and understanding. I use economic estimation—currently centered on inflation—as a concrete setting for asking a broader systems question: how can heterogeneous, partially informed agents learn from one another without assuming an omniscient coordinator?

The primary contribution I am working toward is therefore not an economic forecast. It is an interpretable framework for studying distributed estimation, information complementarity, network structure, and the relationship between individual and group intelligence.

Questions I am exploring

  • What can an agent infer from a small set of locally available signals?
  • Which neighbors possess genuinely complementary information or knowledge?
  • When does consultation improve an individual estimate, and when does it merely reproduce redundant or unreliable information?
  • How does communication topology change both the average group outcome and the distribution of capability across agents?

From private cognition to collective estimation

The platform organizes the research logic into four connected stages. The stages make it possible to separate what an agent knows alone from what becomes possible through structured interaction.

01

Private Cognition

Each agent forms a local estimator from its own limited set of economic signals. This private baseline preserves the heterogeneity of the population.

02

Social Consultation

An agent consults selected neighbors through a designed communication graph, gaining access to targeted estimates or information rather than a complete global state.

03

Knowledge Assimilation

Received knowledge is evaluated and selectively incorporated according to its reliability, complementarity, and relevance to the receiving agent.

04

Collective Estimation

Private and post-consultation outcomes are compared at both the individual and population levels to examine where collective intelligence emerges—and where it does not.

Information propagation & network topology

A network determines which knowledge can become accessible, but access does not imply automatic adoption. OWN2 treats topology, consultation, and assimilation as distinct mechanisms: connections create opportunities to communicate; agent-level judgment determines whether received information is useful; collective metrics reveal how these local choices accumulate across the population.

OWN2 Collective Studio

OWN2 translates the framework into a hands-on research sandbox. Visitors can create and configure agents, assign local economic signals, draw a communication graph, and compare what the system produces before and after consultation.

  • Define heterogeneous agents and their local information.
  • Construct communication links and experiment with network structure.
  • Run private, consulted, assimilated, and collective estimation stages.
  • Compare individual and group-level outcomes.
  • Save and submit a design for later comparison or discussion.

How to Interpret, What to Use, and What to Forward: Decentralized Information Interpretation and Selection for Multi-Agent Global-State Estimation

Zhishan Wang, Zhengyang Liu, and Rong Su

AAMAS 2027 · Research Paper Track

Decentralized estimation · Information selection · Belief packets · Submodular coverage · Multi-hop communication

Request the manuscript →Related research →

A manuscript copy is available on request.

Abstract

We study decentralized information selection in multi-agent estimation with heterogeneous partial information, imperfect local reasoning, and limited communication. We separate local use from recipient-specific forwarding and represent beliefs as immutable packets that may propagate over multiple hops. Each agent evaluates packets by their ability to resolve remaining state ambiguity, yielding a monotone submodular weighted-coverage objective with local Greedy guarantees. Online likelihood adaptation further refines how agents interpret received beliefs. Synthetic and real-data experiments show that selective communication improves estimation over private reasoning, with multi-hop relay and adaptive interpretation contributing substantially to the gains. A macroeconomic case study with 30 agents and 22 distributed signals demonstrates historical U.S. inflation estimation and illustrates how heterogeneous local information can be interpreted, selected, and propagated toward a shared global objective without centralized aggregation. The framework also provides a general information-processing structure with potential applications to social, economic, swarm, and other multi-agent networks under local communication constraints.

The central idea

Separate the interpretation, local use and recipient-specific forwarding of information in a network of heterogeneous agents.

01 · Preserve the evidence

Represent a belief as an immutable packet with source information, so forwarding does not turn copies into new independent evidence.

02 · Separate use from forwarding

An agent can forward evidence that is valuable to a neighbor even when it adds little to its own estimate.

03 · Select by remaining ambiguity

A weighted-coverage formulation gives local greedy selection guarantees, while online likelihood adaptation improves packet interpretation.

Evidence & interpretation

The manuscript combines an analytic example, controlled synthetic studies and a historical U.S. inflation case with 30 agents and 22 distributed signals. The experimental design isolates multi-hop relay, adaptive interpretation and selective communication through matched baselines and ablations.

What the result does—and does not—establish

The greedy guarantee concerns the stated coverage objective, not a universal guarantee of lower realized estimation error. Statistical interpretation depends on the likelihood model and the available evidence. This is a submitted manuscript, not an accepted publication.

Next questions

Study task-dependent information value, capability constraints and the relationship between selected communication and downstream decision quality.

Abstract

Multi-agent coordination often relies on explicit communication, yet agents also reveal task-relevant information through their physical behavior. This paper studies whether relations among multiple observed behaviors can reveal information that is unavailable from each behavior alone, and whether such information can improve the observer’s own decision. We formulate a behavior-mediated coordination framework that connects local observation, joint inference, mission/role selection, and physical execution. In a football-inspired model, we show that two individually uninformative motions can become jointly informative about a latent tactical opportunity, and derive conditions under which this information changes the observer’s mission from waiting to active coverage. Closed-loop simulations show that the resulting decision gain can persist under physical dynamics. A ten-agent formation study further shows that motion-based reasoning improves over delayed reaction, while providing no additional benefit over a strong position-only baseline in the tested settings. These results distinguish behavioral identifiability, decision value, and closed-loop coordination benefit.

The central idea

Joint teammate behaviors can reveal task information that is absent from each behavior alone, but information gain and coordination gain must be tested separately.

01 · Relations carry information

Two motion observations may each be uninformative about a hidden opportunity while their relation distinguishes it.

02 · Information changes a mission

The inferred opportunity affects the choice between waiting and active coverage under an explicit decision criterion.

03 · Close the inference–control loop

Evaluate whether the decision advantage survives physical dynamics, and compare it with strong observation-matched baselines.

Evidence & interpretation

A football-inspired study links joint behavioral inference to WAIT-to-COVER decisions and physical execution. A ten-agent formation experiment provides a useful boundary: motion-based reasoning improves over delayed reaction, but brings no additional benefit over the strong position-only baseline in the tested settings.

What the result does—and does not—establish

No explicit runtime messages are exchanged for the coordination decisions studied. Shared prior structure and local behavior models may still be available. The result is about the specified models and tested baselines, not a general claim that observing actions always improves teamwork.

Next questions

Connect behavior inference to unknown feedback models, changing missions and decision-sufficient understanding in unfamiliar teams.

Task-Conditioned Distributed Relational Formation Control Under a Global Formation Objective

Zhishan Wang and Rong Su

2026 19th International Conference on Control, Automation, Robotics and Vision · Paper 361

Formation control · Multi-agent systems · Distributed control · Soccer tactics · Distributed estimation

View accepted manuscript PDF ↗

Manuscript status. The linked file is the accepted manuscript, submitted version. Its review-system header remains visible; it is not the final IEEE-formatted version. The paper has been accepted for presentation at ICARCV 2026. Final proceedings details and DOI will be added when available.

Abstract overview

This paper proposes a distributed formation-control framework for multi-agent systems under a task-conditioned global objective. The desired formation is separated into internal relational geometry and collective centroid placement, allowing agents to act using local measurements and neighbor communication. Role-specific convex feasible regions are incorporated through projected local updates. Under stated connectivity, feasibility, initialization, and gain conditions, the coupled estimator-controller closed loop has a unique equilibrium and converges geometrically. An 11-player soccer simulation illustrates full-team formation recovery while respecting role constraints and driving both formation and centroid-estimation errors toward zero.

The question behind the paper

Relative measurements are powerful for preserving shape, but they cannot reveal a common translation of the entire formation: if every agent moves by the same vector, all relative displacements remain unchanged. A task, however, may require both the correct internal relationships and the correct collective placement. The paper asks how agents can recover both quantities without giving every agent the full global configuration.

Core contributions

01

A task-conditioned representation

The reference separates a centered canonical formation from its task-dependent centroid, orientation, and scale.

02

A distributed realization

Agents combine local relational errors with a neighbor-based estimate of the evolving team centroid and enforce role feasibility by projection.

03

A coupled convergence guarantee

A comparison-system argument links formation and estimator errors and provides sufficient gain conditions for geometric convergence.

How the theory is built

The derivation moves from a global formation description to local executable updates. These five steps summarize the logic; the manuscript contains the assumptions and full proofs.

01 · REFERENCE

Encode the task-conditioned target

The common objective is ηr = (cr, ψr, ℓr). A centered template q̄σ is transformed into the world frame:

qr = 1n ⊗ cr + (In ⊗ Tr)q̄σ.
02 · DECOMPOSITION

Separate shape from translation

Consensus and disagreement projectors split formation error into orthogonal collective and internal components:

‖p − qr‖² = n‖cp − cr‖² + ‖p⊥ − q⊥r‖².
03 · OBJECTIVE

Build one strongly convex target

The relational-shape and centroid costs are combined. Graph connectivity makes the Hessian positive definite, so the feasible problem has the unique minimizer qr:

Jσ(p) = ½(p − qr)⊤Hσ(p − qr).
04 · DISTRIBUTED CONTROL

Replace the unavailable centroid locally

Each agent maintains ĉi, exchanges estimates with communication neighbors, and applies a projected local update using relational errors and its centroid estimate:

pi+ = ΠXᵢσ[pi − αgidist].
05 · CONVERGENCE

Couple and bound the two errors

Formation error εp and centroid-estimation error εc form a two-dimensional comparison system. Under the stated step-size bound, its nonnegative matrix is Schur stable, giving geometric convergence to (qr, 0).

Two graphs, two responsibilities

The framework deliberately distinguishes two network structures:

  • Formation graph Gfσ: defines which desired spatial relations are controlled.
  • Communication graph Gc: defines how agents exchange centroid estimates.
  • The graphs may differ, but both are fixed, undirected, and connected in the nominal analysis.

This separation makes the information role of each edge explicit and prevents tactical geometry from being conflated with communication architecture.

From a distorted state to the target formation

The controller never needs the complete stacked configuration or the global system matrices. Each agent uses its own position, locally measured tactical-neighbor displacements, stored task information, communication with its neighbors, and its local centroid estimate. Projection keeps every update inside the role-dependent feasible region.

Numerical validation

The numerical study uses an 11-agent 4-2-3-1 formation on a regulation-size field. The initial configuration is deliberately distorted but feasible. Distinct formation and communication graphs are used, and each player is assigned a rectangular role-feasible region.

The agents recover the prescribed positions without leaving their feasible regions. Formation error and centroid-estimation error decay consistently with the paper’s convergence theorem.

Scope & Future work

The accepted paper studies distributed execution under a fixed task mode and a fixed common formation reference. It does not claim autonomous task generation, opponent-aware switching, collision avoidance, or general time-varying and directed-network guarantees.

Future work will extend this foundation toward task-switching and event-triggered formation control. A broader research direction is to connect mission interpretation, distributed task allocation, reference generation, and low-level control so that the collective objective itself can adapt rather than remain externally prescribed.
